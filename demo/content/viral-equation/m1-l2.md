# Why Some Songs Refuse to Die

The previous lesson developed a population-level account of diffusion: affective arousal raises the probability that a recipient shares, the resulting adoption follows the Bass S-curve, and cascades are sustained by a critical mass of receptive nodes rather than by exceptional initiators. That framework tells us *how many* people end up adopting an artifact, and *over what timeframe*. It is silent on a subtler question: what makes a particular artifact *lodge* — fix itself in a listener's mind, get hummed at the bus stop, transmit forward without further effort by the person who heard it first?

This lesson takes up that question through three lenses: the cognitive science of involuntary musical imagery, the role of choreographed movement as redundant memory encoding, and a hypothesis about why low-skill dances outperform virtuosic ones in cultural transmission. The lesson closes with a worked example — a single 1987 release whose chart performance we will model, in the next module, against the framework developed here.

## 1. The Earworm as Cognitive Vector

The technical term for "a song stuck in your head" is *involuntary musical imagery* (INMI), and the most rigorous study of which songs become INMI is Jakubowski, Finkel, Stewart, and Müllensiefen (2017), published in *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*. The team surveyed roughly three thousand participants between 2010 and 2013 about the songs that most frequently lodged in their heads, then matched a top-100 INMI set against a 100-song control group equated for chart popularity, recency, and style. Eighty-three melodic features were compared.

Three of them predicted INMI status with significant effect sizes:

1. **Tempo.** INMI tunes were faster on average than matched controls — fast enough that the listener could plausibly move to them.
2. **Common global contour.** The overall pitch trajectory of the melody — the sequence of rises and falls across the phrase — tended to follow shapes that are statistically common in the broader Western pop corpus. The chorus melody of Maroon 5's "Moves Like Jagger," cited in the study, follows the prototypical rise-then-fall arc.
3. **Unusual interval structure.** Within an otherwise familiar melodic shape, the songs contained at least one feature that violated expectation — an unexpected leap, a repeated note where the listener anticipated movement, an interval longer than the corpus norm.

The authors propose that these features together produce a melody that is easy to retrieve (familiar contour) but distinctive enough to be tagged as itself rather than blending into the listener's general musical background (unusual interval). Subsequent work has reinforced a related interpretation: the songs that lodge are also the songs that are *easy to sing*, which suggests that motor rehearsal — the listener subvocally reproducing the tune — is part of how the lodging happens.

## 2. Choreography as Redundant Encoding

If subvocal rehearsal is one vector for musical memory, full-body movement is a stronger one. A song with an associated dance recruits at least three memory channels rather than one: auditory (the melody), motor (the movement sequence), and visual (the appearance of the movement, whether one's own or another's). Each channel is independently re-cuable. Hearing the song triggers the dance; seeing the dance triggers the song; performing the movement, even partially, can trigger both.

The cognitive-neuroscience literature on the auditory-motor coupling underlying this phenomenon is large and not without controversy. Early popular claims about "mirror neurons" mediating imitation in humans have been substantially walked back since the 2010s, and the current consensus emphasizes a distributed network of auditory, premotor, and parietal regions rather than any single cell type. The behavioral observation, however, is robust: associating a melody with a movement sequence improves recall of both relative to either alone, and the improvement persists over delays of weeks to months.

The implication for diffusion is direct. A song-with-dance is not a single artifact spreading through the population; it is a bundle of auditory, motor, and visual content, any element of which can re-prime the others. The Bass model's assumption that what spreads in week 100 is what spread in week 1 is approximately right for the auditory component, but understates how thoroughly the dance, once attached, becomes a separate vector with its own diffusion characteristics.

## 3. The Choreographic Zipf's Law

A further hypothesis is worth stating, even though it is less formally established than the Jakubowski findings. Across a wide range of viral choreographies — the Macarena, the dougie, the *Gangnam Style* horse-step, the floss, more recently any number of TikTok dances — a striking commonality is that the *core* movement sequence is composed of moves any non-dancer can execute on first attempt. The choreographic vocabulary is small. The skill floor is low. The signature gestures are legible at distance, on small screens, and through poor video compression.

The hypothesis is that this is not accidental. A movement sequence that requires technical training is, by construction, harder to mimic; harder to mimic means lower per-encounter transmission probability; lower transmission probability means a smaller cascade, all else equal. The dances that travel are the dances that survive contact with kitchens, school hallways, and wedding receptions where the participants have had two drinks and no instruction. We might call this the *choreographic Zipf's law* by loose analogy with the linguistic phenomenon: a small vocabulary of high-frequency moves does most of the cultural work, while technically demanding choreography accumulates in the long tail.

## 4. A Worked Example: The 1987 Case

Consider, as a concrete instance, a single popular release from the second half of 1987. We will defer the artifact's identification to the next module and treat it here only as a worked example. The case is well-suited to the framework developed in this lesson and the previous one for several reasons.

**Production.** The track was recorded at a London studio operating in what was then the dominant mode of British dance-pop production: a small group of producer-songwriters working largely with programmed instrumentation rather than session players, turning out chart releases at the rate of one or two per month. The drum sound is the clattering, gated-snare signature produced by the era's standard drum-machine and sequencer hardware. The bass is a synthesizer figure rather than an electric instrument, with the characteristic octave-jump pattern that defines the period's hi-NRG-adjacent dance-pop. The chorus features stacked female backing vocals layered above the lead. The tempo sits in the low one-hundred-tens of beats per minute — fast enough, by Jakubowski's criterion, to qualify as a candidate INMI tune.

**Vocal characteristics.** The lead vocal exhibits one of the period's more-discussed mismatches: a slight-framed young man producing an unexpected baritone. Listeners encountering the audio without the video routinely assumed the singer was older and physically larger than he in fact was. The voice the body did not visually predict.

**Visual presentation.** The accompanying music video, shot in standard mid-1980s broadcast format, presents the singer in a knee-length trench coat over high-waisted dark trousers. The most visually distinctive feature is a notable shock of red hair, neatly cut. The set is minimal; the lighting is direct; the cinematography is shaped by the low-resolution display devices on which most viewers would actually see it.

**Choreography.** This is where the framework applies most directly. The video's signature movement vocabulary is extraordinarily compact: a lateral side-step shuffle with weight transfer on the off-beat; a deliberate index-finger point at chest height, directed at camera; a hand-to-chest gesture aligned with the chorus's downbeats; a quarter-turn-and-look-back executed without footwork flourish. Every gesture is reproducible by a viewer in a domestic kitchen on first attempt. None requires technical training. All are legible at the resolution of a 1987 cathode-ray television set.

**Lyrical structure.** The chorus consists almost entirely of monosyllabic promises clustered around commitment, fidelity, and refusal-to-deceive. Six negation-anchored verbs cover the semantic field of abandonment, deception, desertion, and tears. The chorus is also, by the standards of the corpus, extraordinarily redundant: a single proposition is restated five different ways across the eight-bar phrase. Listeners cannot mishear it. Listeners cannot ignore it. It satisfies the Jakubowski criteria — common global contour, fast tempo, distinctive interval feature in the leap from the verse register into the chorus — and it satisfies them while pairing the auditory channel with a dance vocabulary that any kitchen can host.

**First-wave performance.** The track reached number one in twenty-five countries during 1987 and 1988, including the United Kingdom (where it spent five weeks at the top and was the year's best-selling single), the United States, and West Germany. It won Best British Single at the 1988 BPI Awards. Then, as such releases do, it faded into the long catalog tail of late-1980s dance-pop. The Bass model, fit to the 1987–88 chart-position data, produces a reasonable parameter triple and a reasonable saturation curve.

This is where, in any ordinary case, the analysis would end.

In the next module we name this case and stress-test the framework against its empirical history, which turns out to be considerably stranger than the 1987 data alone would suggest.
