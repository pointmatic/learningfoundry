# The Rick Astley Phenomenon

While you may have already been "Rickrolled" before, the history on that term is not well known. 

![Rick-Rollin'](figures/anomaly-in-action.webp "You've been Rickrolled!")

## An Internet Legend

The song is "Never Gonna Give You Up." The performer is Rick Astley, an English singer from Newton-le-Willows, Lancashire, born in 1966 and barely twenty-one years old at the time of release. The track was recorded on New Year's Day 1987 and released by RCA Records on 27 July 1987 as the lead single from Astley's debut album, *Whenever You Need Somebody*. It was written and produced by the Stock Aitken Waterman team — Mike Stock, Matt Aitken, and Pete Waterman — operating out of their PWL studio in south London, which during the late 1980s was the dominant hit-factory in British dance-pop and the source of the production fingerprint described in the previous lesson. The accompanying music video, presented in the prior content section, was directed by Simon West. It is one of the most-viewed pieces of video content in the history of the public internet.

## Fitting the Framework to the First Wave

Reading the chart trajectory of "Never Gonna Give You Up" through the framework of the previous module produces a tidy result. The single entered the UK Singles Chart in August 1987, climbed to number one within three weeks, held the top position for five consecutive weeks, and finished as the year's best-selling UK single. It reached number one in twenty-five countries during 1987 and 1988, including — after a slow build that included extensive play at New York's Paradise Garage by resident DJ Larry Levan — the United States *Billboard* Hot 100 on 12 March 1988. It won Best British Single at the 1988 BPI Awards. Cumulative single sales by the end of the chart cycle exceeded one million in the UK alone.

A Bass model fit to the country-by-country chart-position data over the eighteen-month window from August 1987 through January 1989 returns parameter estimates well within the range Bass himself reported for consumer durables: a small $p$ (low independent-discovery rate, which makes sense for a debut artist with no prior catalog), a substantially larger $q$ (the dominant mode is imitation — radio play, club play, peer recommendation), and an $m$ corresponding roughly to the size of the global late-1980s English-language pop-music market. The fit is good. The S-curve is the textbook S-curve. The peak occurs at the time predicted by the closed-form expression. The saturation tail decays at the predicted rate.

By the framework of Module 1, this would be a closed case — a successful but unremarkable late-1980s dance-pop release. Astley released a follow-up single, "Together Forever," which also went to number one on the *Billboard* Hot 100; the album sold over fifteen million copies worldwide; he was nominated for a Grammy for Best New Artist; he kept working through the early 1990s and then, in 1993, retired from the music industry at age twenty-seven to raise his daughter. The first wave fits. The framework holds.

The second wave does not.

In the next lesson we examine what happened twenty years later, why the Bass model has no term that can produce it, and what kind of revision the framework requires to accommodate a case in which the same artifact diffuses twice — once as itself, and a second time as the vehicle for a meaning it never originally carried.
