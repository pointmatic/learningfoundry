# The Perfect Bait and Switch

The Bass model fitted to "Never Gonna Give You Up" in 1989 would have predicted, for the years following, a long quiet decay: residual radio play in oldies rotation, occasional appearances on greatest-hits compilations, gradual disappearance from active cultural circulation. For approximately seventeen years that is exactly what happened. Astley retired from music in 1993. The single dropped out of regular airplay through the 1990s. By the early 2000s, the song was a recognizable but unremarkable artifact of late-1980s British dance-pop, in roughly the same cultural position as a thousand other tracks from that era.

This lesson examines what happened next, why no parameterization of the Bass model can produce the resulting curve, and what revision the framework requires.

## 1. The Genealogy of the Second Wave

The second wave begins not in pop music but in an internet word-filter joke. In late 2006, the moderator of the imageboard 4chan — Christopher "moot" Poole — implemented a small site-wide filter that automatically replaced the word *egg* with the word *duck* in user posts. The filter turned *eggroll* into *duckroll*. Within weeks an anonymous user posted a photoshopped image of a mallard duck with truck wheels in place of feet. The image — and the term *duckroll* — became the basis for a bait-and-switch convention: a post with a sensational title would link to the duck-on-wheels image, and the victim was said to have been *duckrolled*.

In May 2007, the trailer for the video game *Grand Theft Auto IV* was released, and the heavy traffic crashed the publisher's servers. Mirrors of the trailer were posted across the internet. On 15 May 2007, a 4chan user named Shawn Cotter — a nineteen-year-old US Air Force airman stationed in Korea, posting under the YouTube username *cotter548* — uploaded the music video for "Never Gonna Give You Up" under the title "Rickroll'D" and posted the link on 4chan's */v/* video games board, claiming it was the trailer. Users clicked. The duckroll convention shifted to a rickroll convention within days.

By February 2008, the convention had escaped 4chan: members of Anonymous played the song outside Church of Scientology offices during Project Chanology protests, in what *The Guardian* called "a live rick-rolling of the Church of Scientology." On 1 April 2008, YouTube redirected every featured video on its front page to the music video for one day; by SurveyUSA's estimate, at least eighteen million American adults had been rickrolled by that month. On 27 November 2008, Astley himself appeared during the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, emerging from a *Foster's Home for Imaginary Friends* float on which he had not been listed and lip-syncing the chorus to live national television. At the 2008 MTV Europe Music Awards, internet-mobilized voting pushed Astley to a Best Act Ever award won over Madonna, U2, and the Beatles. By July 2021, the official YouTube upload had passed one billion views.

This is not a Bass curve. No fit of $(p, q, m)$ produces a saturation, an eighteen-year dormancy, and a second monotonic-growth phase decoupled in time and platform from the first. The first wave was diffusion of a song through the late-1980s pop-music distribution system. The second wave is something the framework does not have a term for.

## 2. What Actually Diffused the Second Time

The first observation worth making is that what spread in 2007–2008 was not exactly the same artifact that spread in 1987. In 1987 the artifact was a song one bought, played, danced to, or heard on the radio. In 2007 the artifact was a song one *unwillingly received in lieu of expected content*. The audiovisual content was unchanged. The pragmatic context was inverted. The 1987 listener wanted the song; the 2007 victim wanted the *Grand Theft Auto IV* trailer and got the song instead.

That asymmetry matters because the proximate cause of further spread is no longer the listener's appreciation of the artifact (Berger and Milkman's high-arousal positive affect attached to the music itself) but the listener's amusement at the *deception* (high-arousal positive affect of a different kind, attached to a different object). The song is the vehicle. The meaning is the cargo. They diffuse through the same network but they are not the same thing.

## 3. Competition for Limited Attention

A useful piece of theoretical machinery for thinking about the second wave is Weng, Flammini, Vespignani, and Menczer (2012), published in *Scientific Reports*. The authors propose an agent-based model in which memes compete for the finite attention of users on a social network. Their central finding — counterintuitive at the time and well-replicated since — is that the heterogeneous popularity and persistence of memes does not require differences in their *intrinsic quality*. Even when all memes in the model are assigned identical intrinsic value, the combination of network structure and limited attention is sufficient to produce the observed empirical distribution: a few memes go viral while most fade.

The implication for our case is that we should not be looking for some special hidden property of "Never Gonna Give You Up" — a sonic feature, a demographic accident — that uniquely explains the second wave. The Weng et al. result says that in a network with finite attention, *some* artifact had to occupy the niche that the rickroll filled. The contingent fact is that this artifact did. The structural fact is that the niche existed. Astley's video had several useful properties for the role: it was visually arresting in a slightly ridiculous way, the chorus was famously redundant (recall the Lesson 1.2 analysis), the song was old enough to be unfamiliar to teenagers but recent enough to be retrievable, and — critically — the lyrical content created a piquant contrast with the deceptive context in which it was being deployed. But none of these properties is sufficient. They combine with the network-structural facts described by Weng et al. to produce the observed cascade.

## 4. A Two-Layer Sketch

We can give the qualitative dynamics a more explicit form. Let $A(t)$ denote the cumulative number of distinct encounters with the audiovisual artifact (the music video itself), and let $D(t)$ denote the cumulative number of *deceptive* deployments of the artifact — instances where the artifact was used as a Rickroll bait-and-switch. By construction $D(t) \le A(t)$, since every Rickroll involves an encounter, but the inverse is not true: many artifact encounters in 1987 were not deceptive deployments, and many in 2008 were.

A teaching-grade sketch of their coupled dynamics is:

$$\frac{dA}{dt} \;=\; \underbrace{\bigl(p_A + q_A \tfrac{A}{m_A}\bigr)\bigl(m_A - A\bigr)}_{\text{baseline Bass diffusion}} \;+\; \underbrace{\kappa\,\frac{dD}{dt}}_{\text{deception-driven encounters}}$$

The first term is the Bass model from the previous module: the song's organic diffusion. The second term is the additional contribution from the parasitic memetic layer — every new Rickroll event, by construction, produces an artifact encounter that the baseline term does not predict. The coupling coefficient $\kappa$ measures how efficiently the parasitic layer back-feeds the host artifact's reach. For "Never Gonna Give You Up" between 2007 and 2021, $\kappa$ is empirically large; the parasitic layer is responsible for the substantial majority of the artifact's accumulated YouTube views.

This is, as any introductory two-layer model must concede, a teaching simplification. The dynamics of $D(t)$ are themselves complicated. They depend on the *novelty* of the prank — each victim must be at least partly naive about the convention, which means $D$ has a separate saturation determined by the population of internet users who have not yet learned to recognize a Rickroll bait link. They depend on platform shifts — the migration from 4chan to YouTube to Twitter to TikTok each reset the receptive-node distribution. And they depend on cultural-symbolic factors that the framework treats as exogenous: the model does not capture, for instance, why public figures and institutions periodically participate (the United States Congress YouTube launch in January 2009; Oregon state legislators in 2011; the official White House Twitter account in 2011). These institutional rickrolls are a separate phenomenon driven by signaling rather than by deception, and a more complete model would treat them as a third layer.

The qualitative shape, however, is correct. The second wave is not anomalous in the sense of violating the laws of cultural diffusion. It is anomalous in the sense of revealing that the framework of Module 1 — single artifact, fixed meaning, bounded $m$ — is a special case rather than the general one. Cultural artifacts can carry payloads. Payloads can re-host. The reproduction number of an artifact-plus-payload can exceed the reproduction number of either alone.

## 5. What the Framework Now Predicts — and What It Cannot

Returning to the assumptions flagged at the close of Lesson 1.1: the framework treated the artifact as fixed and the market potential as bounded. Both assumptions are now visibly wrong for "Never Gonna Give You Up." The artifact has been recoded — what spreads in 2008 carries a meaning ("you have been deceived; the joke is on you and the joke is on me too") that the 1987 artifact did not carry. And $m$, which seemed in 1989 to be the size of the late-1980s English-language pop market, turns out to have been only the bound on the *first-layer* market; the second layer's $m$ is the size of the global internet population that can be exposed to a YouTube link, a much larger and still-growing quantity.

What the framework does *not* tell us is what any of this has been like for the person on the cover. The performer who walked away from music in 1993 was, two decades later, the unintended host of the world's most famous bait-and-switch. He has had a second career. He has had things to say about all of this. The next module — and the final one — turns from the phenomenon to the person.
